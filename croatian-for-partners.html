Croatian for families

Learning Croatian for your partner's family and friends

By Marina Toto, Croatian tutor in Kastav near Rijeka · September 2026

Your partner is Croatian. There are summer visits, grandparents who don't speak English, friends who fall back into Croatian after the first round of drinks, family lunches that last four hours, and maybe a child who switches languages mid-sentence. You don't need perfect grammar for that. You need to be part of the conversation instead of waiting for the translated version.

Lessons are in English, so it doesn't matter whether you're from Italy, the Netherlands or Canada. What matters is what you want to be able to say, and to whom.

What you actually need

A textbook starts with ordering coffee. Family life needs something else:

  • Small talk with your in-laws and your partner's friends – how are you, how was the trip, yes, I'd love more food (you will be offered more food).
  • Following the table conversation – people talk fast, over each other, and switch topics every minute. Understanding the gist is the first big win.
  • Phone and video calls – short, practical exchanges with grandparents, without your partner translating every sentence.
  • Holidays and traditions – Christmas Eve, Easter, name days, weddings: the phrases and customs that come with them.
  • Your child's world – understanding what your kid picks up from grandma, and joining in instead of being left out.

Croatian has a word for everyone in the family

English says "mother-in-law". Croatian wants to know whose mother:

punica · punacyour wife's mother · father
svekrva · svekaryour husband's mother · father
šogor · šogoricabrother-in-law · sister-in-law (colloquial)
nono · nonagrandpa · grandma in Istria and Kvarner

That last one is a good example of why family Croatian is its own thing: what relatives say at home often isn't what the textbook teaches. Around Rijeka you'll also hear "ča" instead of "što" (what). I teach both the standard language and what you'll actually hear at the table.

Why your partner can't be your teacher

It sounds like the obvious solution, and it almost never works. Ask a Croatian why something is said a certain way and the honest answer is usually "that's just how it is" – they've never had to think about it. Add tiredness, a toddler and the tenth correction of the evening, and it quickly becomes a source of tension rather than progress.

A better division of roles: you learn the structure with me, and you practise with your partner and family. They get to be the people you talk to, not the people who correct you.

Keeping up with a bilingual child

Children pick up Croatian from family surprisingly fast, and at some point the parent who doesn't speak it starts missing things: songs, jokes, what grandma just said. Learning alongside your child is one of the best motivations there is, and it sends a strong message at home that both languages matter.

How lessons work

  • 1-on-1, online, in English – on Zoom or Google Meet, at times that fit around family life.
  • Built around your family – the situations, people and topics that actually come up for you.
  • Ahead of a visit – we can focus on what's coming: a wedding, Christmas, a first meeting with the relatives.
  • From someone who's been there – I spent eight years building a life and career in Berlin in a language that wasn't mine, so I know what it's like to sit at a table and follow only half.
